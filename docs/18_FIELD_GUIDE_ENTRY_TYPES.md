# Field Guide Entry Types

## Purpose

Define the approved functional entry types for the **Muscular America Field Guide**.

Entry types describe how a piece of content functions for the reader. They are not subject categories.

Content domains such as recovery, nutrition, strength, mobility, gym conduct, conditioning, and goal setting live in section organization and tags. They do not become entry types.

## Status

```text
PROVISIONAL — FOUNDER APPROVAL REQUIRED
```

Once approved, this document becomes the canonical specification for Field Guide entry types.

## Governing Principles

The entry-type system must be:

- small enough for readers to understand intuitively
- flexible enough to support the entire Field Guide
- usable in print and online
- distinct in function
- free of gimmicky naming
- visually adaptable
- concise
- independent from other publishers’ terminology
- suitable for beginners, returning trainees, and experienced trainees

Avoid a taxonomy that requires readers to study the taxonomy. The system should feel natural through repeated use.

## Entry-Type System at a Glance

| Entry Type | Primary Function | Typical Reader Question |
|---|---|---|
| Quick Principle | State one durable idea worth remembering | What should I remember? |
| Gym Signal | Notice a gym-environment or shared-space condition | What should I notice here? |
| Training Rationale | Explain why a practice is useful | Why are we doing this? |
| Common Drift | Recognize a quiet pattern of deviation | Where might this quietly go off course? |
| Try This | Offer one low-friction action to test | What can I test or apply next? |
| Checkpoint | Pause for brief self-review | What should I check before continuing? |
| Goal Marker | Frame direction across time horizons | What am I moving toward? |
| Side Note | Add a useful supporting detail | What useful detail sits beside this? |

## Quick Principle

### Purpose

A compact statement of a durable training, nutrition, recovery, or gym-use principle.

### Reader Function

Gives the reader one clear idea that remains useful after the page is closed.

### Typical Length

Very short.

### Best Used For

- Single durable ideas
- Cross-level truths that do not depend on one program
- Standalone reminders that can appear in indexes or pull quotes

### Do Not Use For

- Multi-step instructions
- Long explanations of why
- Slogans, hype, or motivational catchphrases
- Content whose main value is an immediate experiment

### Structural Pattern

1. Principle statement  
2. Optional one-line clarification  
3. No required action block

### Print Behavior

May stand alone as a short segment. Strong candidate for visual emphasis without becoming a sticker or slogan badge.

### Web Behavior

Useful as a scannable card or anchor in browsing views. May link to a related Training Rationale or Try This, but must remain complete without the link.

### Tone

Direct, calm, non-slogan. Prefer plain sentence form over chant form.

### Example Uses

- “Progression beats novelty when the goal is strength.”
- “If the warm-up is rushed, the first working sets often pay for it.”

## Gym Signal

### Purpose

A short observation about the gym environment, social behavior, equipment use, spatial awareness, or conditions that affect training.

### Reader Function

Helps the reader notice what supports a usable training space — for themselves and for others.

### Typical Length

Very short to short.

### Best Used For

- Reracking weights
- Cleaning equipment before or after use where appropriate
- Crossing or entering active workout zones
- Communicating before entering someone’s working space
- Extended social interruptions
- Equipment availability patterns
- Recognizing when an area is active

### Do Not Use For

- Moral policing or ranking “serious” lifters
- Scolding beginners
- Etiquette theater or rigid rule displays
- Hostility, territoriality, or shaming
- General training principles unrelated to the environment

Courtesy and shared-space respect belong here when the main value is environmental or social awareness. If the main value is a pattern of personal deviation (for example, drifting into long conversations every session), prefer Common Drift.

### Structural Pattern

1. What to notice  
2. Optional practical implication  
3. Optional brief why-it-matters line (keep short; long explanations become Training Rationale)

### Print Behavior

Short, separable segments. Good for glancing while flipping pages. Should not read like a code of conduct poster.

### Web Behavior

May appear near related training entries. Keep the same observational framing; do not escalate into forum-style rules enforcement.

### Tone

Observational and practical. Courtesy as a training-support condition, not moral superiority.

### Example Uses

- “If someone is mid-set between the dumbbells you want, wait for the natural break rather than stepping through their working path.”
- “Reracking is part of finishing the set, not an optional courtesy for later.”

## Training Rationale

### Purpose

A concise explanation of why a practice, exercise choice, sequence, rest period, progression method, or training behavior is useful.

### Reader Function

Answers the “why” so the reader can apply judgment, not only follow steps.

### Typical Length

Short to medium.

### Best Used For

- Cause and purpose
- Distinguishing principle from preference
- Clarifying tradeoffs without demanding one universal method

### Do Not Use For

- One-line slogans (use Quick Principle)
- Immediate experiments (use Try This)
- Presenting one method as universally best
- Dense academic reviews

### Structural Pattern

1. Practice or choice named  
2. Why it tends to help  
3. Optional boundary (“not always,” “depends on…”)  
4. Optional link to evidence class in metadata, not a bibliography dump in the body

### Print Behavior

Slightly longer than most types; still a short encounter, not a chapter. Break visually if needed, but keep one rationale per entry.

### Web Behavior

May expand with a “related reading” link on MuscularAmerica.com. The Field Guide entry itself stays compact; web expansion must not redefine the type.

### Tone

Informed and restrained. Explain without lecturing.

### Example Uses

- Why a few controlled warm-up sets often improve first working-set quality.
- Why rest between hard sets is not “wasted time” when strength is the goal.

## Common Drift

### Purpose

A recognizable way people gradually move away from their intended objective.

### Reader Function

Helps the reader spot quiet off-course patterns before they become habits.

### Typical Length

Short.

### Best Used For

- Standing around talking through the session
- Adding exercises without purpose
- Abandoning progression
- Rushing warm-ups
- Chasing fatigue instead of training quality
- Changing programs too often
- Letting distraction replace focus

### Do Not Use For

- Accusatory or shaming portraits of “people who don’t care”
- One-off mistakes that are not patterns
- Safety emergencies (word those carefully in Checkpoint, Training Rationale, or front-matter scope notes)
- Pure environment cues with no personal deviation pattern (use Gym Signal)

### Structural Pattern

1. Intended objective (implied or stated briefly)  
2. How drift typically appears  
3. Optional what-to-notice cue  
4. No scolding close

### Print Behavior

Works well as a brief “pattern” segment readers can recognize quickly.

### Web Behavior

May pair with a Checkpoint or Try This nearby. Keep the entry observational on its own.

### Tone

Observational, not accusatory. “This often happens” rather than “you are doing it wrong.”

### Example Uses

- Adding “just one more” accessory until the session loses its main lift focus.
- Treating every sore day as a reason to invent a new program.

## Try This

### Purpose

One low-friction action, experiment, or adjustment the reader can apply immediately.

### Reader Function

Turns reading into a small next step without pretending to be individualized programming.

### Typical Length

Very short to short.

### Best Used For

- Single experiments
- Small adjustments with clear scope
- Optional invitations the reader can accept or skip

### Do Not Use For

- Full workouts or programs
- Commands that ignore context, equipment, or ability
- Medical or diagnostic instructions
- Bundles of multiple unrelated actions

### Structural Pattern

1. Invitation (“Try…”, “For one session…”)  
2. The single action  
3. Optional context or stop condition  
4. Optional what-to-notice afterward

### Print Behavior

Should feel optional and light. Avoid checklist formatting that implies obligation.

### Web Behavior

May become an interactive checklist later; the editorial type remains an invitation, not a tracked assignment system.

### Tone

Inviting, practical, limited. Never bossy.

### Example Uses

- “For your next warm-up, keep the first two minutes without headphones and notice whether focus arrives sooner.”
- “Write tomorrow’s main lift before you enter the gym.”

## Checkpoint

### Purpose

A brief self-assessment or pause for review.

### Reader Function

Creates a moment to check whether continuing as-is still makes sense.

### Typical Length

Very short.

### Best Used For

- Form, effort, consistency, recovery, focus, progress, readiness
- Exercise suitability
- Whether the current approach still matches the goal

### Do Not Use For

- Quizzes, scores, or pass/fail tests
- Diagnostic tools or medical screening
- Long evaluation rubrics
- Replacing a Goal Marker when the main value is destination framing

### Structural Pattern

1. Pause cue  
2. One to three plain questions or checks  
3. Optional “if not, adjust” line without prescribing a full plan

### Print Behavior

Visually quiet pause — not a worksheet. Should be readable in seconds.

### Web Behavior

May use expandable prompts later; do not turn Checkpoints into account-based assessments.

### Tone

Calm, non-judgmental. A missed check is information.

### Example Uses

- “Before adding load: is the bar path still under control on the last warm-up?”
- “Has this program had enough consistent weeks to judge, or only enough weeks to get bored?”

## Goal Marker

### Purpose

A compact way to frame progress across short-term goals, mid-term benchmarks, and long-term destinations.

### Reader Function

Helps the reader understand direction: what the marker is, why it matters, how it may vary by experience, and how it can be adjusted.

### Typical Length

Short.

### Best Used For

- Naming a horizon (short / mid / long)
- Showing why the marker guides decisions
- Noting experience-level variation
- Framing a missed marker as information rather than failure

### Do Not Use For

- Full goal-setting curricula
- Shame-based “no excuses” framing
- Universal numeric standards presented as moral ranks
- Session-level self-checks better served by Checkpoint

### Structural Pattern

1. Marker named and horizon stated  
2. Why it helps decision-making  
3. Optional variation by experience level  
4. Optional adjustment note  
5. Optional reminder that missing it is data

### Print Behavior

Segmented and glanceable. Avoid dense tables of standards as the default.

### Web Behavior

May link to calculators or longer articles for expansion. The marker entry stays a framing device, not a dashboard.

### Tone

Practical and respectful. Destinations guide; they do not judge worth.

### Example Uses

- Short-term: show up for three planned sessions this week.
- Mid-term: add a small, repeatable load when form stays stable.
- Long-term: build a strength destination that still fits life constraints.

## Side Note

### Purpose

A brief supporting fact, historical detail, terminology note, equipment observation, cultural note, or useful aside.

### Reader Function

Enriches nearby content without becoming the main event.

### Typical Length

Very short.

### Best Used For

- Terminology clarifications
- Equipment naming or setup observations
- Brief historical or cultural context
- Secondary facts that aid understanding

### Do Not Use For

- Trivia for trivia’s sake
- Novelty as the only value
- Primary principles, actions, or rationales
- Catch-all storage for hard-to-classify drafts

### Structural Pattern

1. The aside  
2. Optional one-line tie-back to the nearby topic  
3. No call to action required

### Print Behavior

Visually subordinate — smaller emphasis than Quick Principle or Goal Marker. Should not compete with the primary entry beside it.

### Web Behavior

May appear as a footnote-style block or secondary panel. Do not use web novelty (animations, popovers) to justify weak content.

### Tone

Light, useful, restrained. Curious without being cute.

### Example Uses

- A brief note on why “machine” and “cable” are not interchangeable in setup cues.
- A short terminology aside defining “working set” in plain language.

## Optional Ninth Type

**Candidate considered:** Safety Note

**Decision:** No ninth type is currently necessary.

Safety and scope limitations can be handled without a dedicated type:

- Front matter and platform disclaimers for general scope
- Optional `caution` (or equivalent) metadata and in-entry caution lines
- Checkpoint for readiness and suitability pauses
- Training Rationale when the “why” includes load, fatigue, or technique risk
- Quick Principle when a durable cautionary idea is the whole point

A standing Safety Note type would risk becoming a generic disclaimer box, creating alarmism, simulating medical advice, or appearing so often that it loses meaning.

If a recurring, visually distinct safety treatment later proves necessary across many entries, propose a ninth type through change control — not for a single difficult entry.

## Removed and Merged Types

These are **not** active Muscular America Field Guide entry types:

| Former type | Decision | Disposition |
|---|---|---|
| Field Note | Removed | Too generic; too closely associated with another publication system |
| Trivia Spot | Renamed | Replaced by **Side Note** |
| Courtesy Note | Merged | Handle through **Gym Signal** (environmental/social awareness) or **Common Drift** (personal deviation pattern) |
| Recovery Note | Removed | Recovery is a content domain, not a content format |

Do not preserve these as active types in structured content.

## Selection Rules

```text
Use Quick Principle when the main value is the principle itself.
Use Training Rationale when the main value is the explanation.
Use Try This when the main value is immediate application.
Use Checkpoint when the main value is self-review.
Use Common Drift when the main value is recognizing a pattern of deviation.
Use Gym Signal when the main value is environmental or social awareness.
Use Goal Marker when the main value is direction or progress framing.
Use Side Note when the content is useful but secondary.
```

Additional rules:

- One entry should have one primary type.
- Secondary tags may describe topic, audience, or evidence.
- Do not combine entry-type names into hybrids (for example, “Try This Checkpoint”).
- Do not assign a type based on visual layout alone.
- Do not use Side Note as a catch-all.
- Do not create new types to solve one isolated content problem.
- Do not promote a content domain (recovery, nutrition, etiquette, strength) into an entry type without review.

If two types seem to fit, choose the one that matches the **dominant reader question**. Put supporting material in optional fields (`rationale`, `action`, `caution`) or a related entry.

## Length and Format Guidance

Provisional ranges — not rigid word counts:

| Entry Type | Provisional length |
|---|---|
| Quick Principle | Very short |
| Gym Signal | Very short to short |
| Training Rationale | Short to medium |
| Common Drift | Short |
| Try This | Very short to short |
| Checkpoint | Very short |
| Goal Marker | Short |
| Side Note | Very short |

Principle:

> The type should determine function before it determines size.

Do not make every entry visually identical. Browsing depends on varied, separable segments — not one uniform card shape for all functions.

## Cross-Platform Use

The same entry type may present differently across surfaces while keeping a stable function:

| Surface | Presentation may change | Function must not |
|---|---|---|
| Printed Field Guide | Compact segments, print hierarchy, limited cross-links | Primary reader question |
| MuscularAmerica.com | Navigation, related links, optional expansion | Same type meaning |
| Newsletter excerpts | Standalone excerpt with light context | Same type meaning |
| Calculator support text | Adjacent explanation near a tool | Type still names editorial function |
| Course materials | Sequencing inside a lesson | Type is not redefined as “lesson step” |

Web features must not redefine the editorial type. A Try This does not become a tracked assignment because a checkbox is available. A Checkpoint does not become a diagnostic score because a form is easy to build.

## Editorial Quality Checks

Before approving an entry:

- Does the entry have one dominant function?
- Is the selected type the clearest fit?
- Does it overlap another entry type unnecessarily?
- Is the language direct and calm?
- Is the entry useful without extra explanation?
- Is the level of certainty appropriate?
- Does it avoid shame, scolding, hype, or false universality?
- Is a content domain being confused with the entry type?
- Could the entry be shortened without losing function?
- Does the type work in both print and web?
- For Gym Signal: is this observation rather than policing?
- For Side Note: is this subordinate and useful, not trivia filler?
- For Goal Marker: is a missed marker framed as information rather than failure?

## Founder Decisions

Provisional decisions recorded for approval:

1. The active system should contain **eight** entry types unless a ninth proves necessary.
2. **Field Note** is not part of the Muscular America system.
3. **Side Note** replaces Trivia Spot.
4. Courtesy content is handled through **Gym Signal** or **Common Drift**.
5. Recovery remains a content domain rather than an entry type.
6. Entry-type names remain provisional until Founder approval.
7. No ninth type (including Safety Note) is currently necessary.

## Change Control

- New entry types require a demonstrated recurring editorial need.
- A single difficult entry is not enough reason to expand the system.
- Renaming a type requires updating this specification and all structured content that uses the name.
- Topic categories must not be promoted into entry types without review.
- Once this document is Founder-approved, material changes should be recorded in `docs/16_EDITORIAL_DECISIONS.md`.
